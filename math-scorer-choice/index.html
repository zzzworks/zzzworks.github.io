Scorer Choice in Math Reasoning Evaluation

TL;DR

On Qwen3 base models, verifier choice alone can swing reported MATH-500 accuracy by 20 percentage points without touching the model. We encountered this while building CRISP, an on-policy self-distillation method for compressing reasoning-model outputs, and we wanted to share what we found because it generalises beyond a single paper. The dominant axis of the gap is not answer-extraction (which our earlier post covered) but symbolic equivalence: responses such as \dfrac{1}{2} vs. \frac{1}{2}, 0.5 vs. \frac{1}{2}, or 2\sqrt{2} vs. \sqrt{8} are all mathematically equivalent but fail under string-comparison scorers and succeed under sympy-based ones. We lay out a four-policy decomposition that pins down where each percentage point comes from.

1. Background: how we got to this question

CRISP (arXiv:2603.05433) is a teacher–student framework for compressing reasoning-model outputs. The teacher is the base model conditioned on a concise system prompt, and the student is trained via reverse-KL to match the teacher's per-token distribution. The objective directly compresses generation length: a typical Qwen3-8B response on MATH-500 drops from ~4,900 tokens at base to ~2,050 tokens at step-100 (a 58% reduction).

While iterating on CRISP we cross-checked accuracy numbers under two scorer implementations: an internal extension of math_dapo (the scoring scripts that ship with the DAPO-Math training corpus), and HuggingFace math_verify (a sympy-backed reference implementation widely used in modern reasoning evaluations such as lm-eval-harness and Open-R1). On the same generated responses, the two scorers disagreed by ~20 percentage points on the Qwen3-{8,14}B MATH-500 baselines.

The puzzle. If both numbers come from the same generated MATH-500 responses, where do 20 percentage points come from?

The rest of this post walks through what we found, which turns out to be more interesting than “the two libraries disagree.” The gap decomposes cleanly into two distinct mechanisms (answer extraction and symbolic equivalence), and the second one — which we believe is under-discussed in the literature — dominates on Qwen3 outputs.

2. The four scorer policies

The same (response, gold_answer) pair can be turned into a score in dramatically different ways. We isolated four policies that span the design space encountered in current evaluation pipelines.

SCORER A — math_dapo (vanilla)

Extract: regex on last 300 chars for Answer: X. Compare: string equality after a fixed normalization rule list (\dfrac→\frac, strip \left/\right, strip $ delimiters, etc.).

Misses ~60–98% of Qwen3 base-model responses because thinking-mode Qwen3 writes \boxed{...} after </think>, not Answer:.

SCORER B — math_dapo + \boxed{} fallback

Extract: SCORER A regex; if no match, also try \boxed{X}. Compare: same string equality after normalization.

The policy implicit in our prior pipeline. The 70–80% numbers in the earlier version of the paper come from this policy.

SCORER C — math_verify single-call

Extract: HuggingFace math_verify's default extraction (LatexExtractionConfig for \boxed{...}, ExprExtractionConfig for anchored expressions). Compare: sympy symbolic equivalence.

A single library call on the full response. Closest to what most modern reasoning-eval pipelines (lm-eval, Open-R1) use out of the box.

SCORER D — math_verify dual-path

Extract: SCORER C, plus a second pass that regex-extracts Answer: X, wraps as \boxed{X}, and runs math_verify on that constructed string. Compare: OR of both passes.

Our final policy. Gives credit for both Qwen3's native \boxed{} format and the DAPO-prompt-requested Answer: X format.

The defining design choices are along two axes:

  1. Extraction — which substring of the response is treated as the model's committed answer?
  2. Comparison — once a substring is extracted, how is it compared to gold? String equality after canonicalisation, or symbolic equivalence via sympy?

Our earlier post focused on the first axis (Answer Format Sensitivity in Qwen3 Math Reasoning Evaluation); this post focuses on the second.

The table is information-dense; the most striking observations:

  1. Reading across, on the base rows: SCORER A reads 2–35% on the base model, while SCORER D reads 70–97%. Same generations.
  2. A → B (still string-equality, only adding the \boxed{} fallback) recovers most of the gap on MATH-500: +59.6 pp on 8B base, +36.7 pp on 14B base. This is the extraction story documented in our earlier post.
  3. B → C (replacing string-equality with sympy symbolic equivalence) recovers another +18.2 pp on 8B-base MATH-500. This is the gap that closes the 70–80% baseline up to 95–97%, and it is the focus of this post.
  4. The step-100 rows tell a different story. SCORER A jumps to 84–85%, SCORER B follows close behind, and SCORER C’s lead narrows. CRISP’s training shifts which scorer is binding (see Section 5).

4. Decomposing the gap between scorer policies

Our earlier verifier used SCORER B (math_dapo's Answer: regex with a \boxed{...} fallback). The base-row SCORER B values (78.15 / 73.33 / 65.42 / 71.73 / 76.67 / 68.75) match the baselines from that verifier within 1–4 pp; SCORER A on the same generations reads 30–75 pp lower. Stepping across the four policies on a single cell pins down where each percentage point originates:

The two largest contributors are the \boxed{} extraction fallback (A → B, +59.60 pp) and the sympy symbolic-equivalence backend (B → C, +18.20 pp). The first arises because Qwen3 emits a boxed answer after </think> and the Answer: regex misses it; this is the subject of our earlier post. The second — the focus of this post — arises because once the answer is extracted, the model's surface form often differs from gold in ways that no fixed string-normalisation table can canonicalise.

5. Training reshapes the model's output format, which then reshapes the scorer's verdict

Reading the table down the columns reveals a second, subtler effect: CRISP doesn't just change accuracy; it changes format, and the change interacts with scorer choice.

5a. Training can shift output format, which interacts with scorer choice

Reading down the columns of the 8B MATH-500 cell, SCORER B reads 78.15% at base and 85.70% at step-100 (+7.55 pp). SCORER C reads 96.35% → 93.00%, and SCORER D reads 97.08% → 96.23%. The trained model has changed something the three scorers respond to differently.

The mechanism is direct. CRISP's teacher is the base model conditioned on a concise system prompt. When Qwen3 is asked to be concise, it strips LaTeX decoration before it strips content:

  • Drops the polished post-</think> answer block (where \dfrac, \left/\right, $...$ typically live).
  • Writes a plain Answer: X line because that's the shortest form satisfying the prompt's instruction.
  • Strips inline math wrappers from numeric answers (0.5 in place of \dfrac{1}{2}; 17 in place of \boxed{17}).

The student inherits this style via reverse-KL. SCORER B's normalisation rule list then succeeds on payloads it previously failed on (a decorated \dfrac{1}{2} that didn't string-match gold \frac{1}{2} becomes the simpler 1/2, which does match after normalisation). SCORER C and SCORER D, which use symbolic equivalence, are largely insensitive to the formatting shift.

This is a generic concern for any training method whose objective indirectly reshapes output format: cross-row comparisons under different scorers can read large gains or large regressions depending on which scorer the reader applies, even when the model's underlying correctness has not changed much. The conservative practice is to apply a single fixed policy to both rows of the comparison.

5b. The C → D gap on step-100 captures prompt-following recovery

A second consequence of the format shift: step-100 responses frequently take the form Answer: X with no accompanying \boxed{X}. The trained model treats the Answer: line as its sole final commitment.

SCORER C (single-call math_verify) cannot extract these unless X is a digit-led number or arithmetic expression — the only forms ExprExtractionConfig's anchored regex (in math_verify/parser.py) matches:

number_re = r"-?\d+(\.\d+)?"
operators = ['+','-','*','/','^','(',')']
expr_re   = r"-?\(?-?\d[\d\.\s+\-*/^()]*[+\-*/^()][\d\.\s+\-*/^()]+\)?"
expr_or_number = (expr_re | number_re)

For MATH-500 text answers (Evelyn, north), multi-choice letters (A, B), and fraction or set expressions written without LaTeX delimiters, SCORER C returns 0 despite the model having followed the prompt's instructional contract verbatim. SCORER D recovers these — 129 / 4000 samples (+3.23 pp) on 8B-step-100 MATH-500, 219 / 4000 (+5.47 pp) on 14B-step-100 MATH-500 — by extracting the Answer: payload, wrapping as \boxed{X}, and reusing math_verify's LatexExtractionConfig on the constructed string.

The crucial point: this C → D recovery only matters after training. On base, the model writes \boxed{X} anyway, and the C and D columns agree to within a percentage point. After CRISP, the model drops the \boxed{} wrapping — and SCORER C, which still expects it, starts penalising responses that perfectly followed the prompt.

6. Implications for evaluating reasoning models

We came to this analysis while reconciling two scorers on a single project, but the result generalises. Verifier choice is a first-order methodological decision in reasoning-model evaluation, not a downstream detail. A 20-pp accuracy gap on identical generated responses can hinge entirely on the scorer's extraction policy (Answer:-regex vs. \boxed{...} extraction vs. both) and on whether equivalence is checked by string match or symbolic computation.

Two specific recommendations for future reasoning-evaluation reports:

  1. Explicitly disclose the verifier. Prompt template, extraction policy, equivalence-checking backend, and library version. "We used math_verify" is ambiguous — single-call or dual-pass? Default ExtractionConfig or custom? A small implementation footnote eliminates ambiguity that can swing the headline by 20 pp.
  2. Apply the verifier identically to all rows of the comparison. A reported "+X pp from training" must use the same scorer policy on the baseline checkpoint and the trained checkpoint. The diagonal under a fixed policy is the only comparison invariant to formatting changes induced by training.

Headline accuracy claims for reasoning-model training methods can otherwise reflect scorer rebasing rather than model improvement, particularly when training changes the model's output format — which, as we've seen, can happen as a side effect of objectives that are not nominally about formatting at all.
